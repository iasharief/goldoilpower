Ancient World Project

Thesis

Throughout history, huge natural wealth has rarely sat in the ground. Across centuries, rulers and states have tried to convert their material riches into something more lasting and powerful. That concept of repurposing has ranged from prestige to influence to cultural reach. Seven hundred years ago, Mansa Musa, the ruler of the Mali Empire, used his hajj to Mecca to display his wealth and power, which brought him and his country international recognition. In the modern era, Saudi Arabia has pursued a similar goal through Vision 2030, a program to diversify the kingdom’s oil economy by expanding its global influence via infrastructure, technology, and sports. Part of their strategy has drawn criticism for covering up poor media coverage and for sportswashing, the use of sports investments to improve a country’s image and build soft power. Undoubtedly, Mansa Musa and Saudi Arabia show how leaders of resource-rich states work to transform their wealth into wider political and cultural power, albeit through different means. To a great extent, the human desire to convert raw wealth into usable political and cultural power is evident over centuries, seen in both Mansa Musa’s hajj and Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030. In both cases, natural resource wealth became powerful because leaders were able to transform it into three key goals of visibility, reputation, and influence.
