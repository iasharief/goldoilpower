TWE Answer

To a great extent, the human desire to convert raw wealth into usable political power remains a prevalent and ingrained aspect of nature in the modern world. Even seven centuries after Mansa Musa’s Hajj elevated the status and international recognition of the Mali empire, the same impulse drives Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 initiative. Both cases show how leaders of resource-rich lands recognize and instinctively act on ways to turn wealth into soft power, diplomatic influence, and overall prestige. In ways, this trait has become even more prevalent in the modern world. In the current era of huge wealth funds, media, and sporting events, the simple spectacle logic that led Mansa Musa to extend the purpose of his Hajj continues to influence political behavior. Nations in the Middle East like Saudi Arabia, continue to invest huge sums of money from natural resources into domestic and international projects because they can exert political influence and reshape the preexisting global perceptions. The persistence of this tendency proves that the impulse to turn wealth into power is a feature of human societies that continues to influence international relations today.
