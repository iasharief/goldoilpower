Comparison

Mansa Musa’s Hajj and Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030, although separated by more than seven hundred years, display many similarities as to how leaders convert raw resource wealth into political and cultural power. While executed in differing strategies, they show how they move past limitations in geography and economy. They both make huge investments to gain visibility worldwide. Musa’s sixty thousand person entourage with “500 servants, each carrying a staff made from more than 4 pounds of gold” and “100 camels–each carrying 300 pounds of gold” mirrors saudis $480 million summer spending on soccer players (benzema, ronaldo, kante, mane) on top of Liv golf’s hundred of millions for contracts to the best pro golfers, and the the overall ten billion push that “catapult[ed] them among the biggest spenders in global football.” In both cases, they are shaping their reputation. They use underground riches like Mali’s gold and Saudi Arabia’s oil exports into non-tangible assets that can be used to gain “soft power”. This power can be used to attract talent, trade, and global recognition. On top of that, both empires looked to create and invest in cultural and economic hubs. Musa recruited al-Sahili to build Mosques andHoly sites, and laid the foundation for a university. Musa’s expenditure highlights how PIF’s investments look to create a stake in more than keeping money around. PIF invested $100 billion for AI with Blackstone, $30 billion for EA, among EV investments.

These investments mirror Mali’s projects to create lasting influence and power in the future, even providing funds to competing markets (EV vs Oil industry) to diversify their economy. Another similarity is that neither Mali nor Saudi Arabia turned wealth into power alone. In Saudi Arabia’s case, their sportswashing only works because other groups, such as leagues, clubs, and sports organizations, are willing to take part for their own financial gain and attention. Mansa Musa had a similar case with Egypt as a whole. Cairo's merchants and court benefited greatly from the wealth Mali brought, while Mali gained diplomatic relations, more recognition, and access to Islamic scholars. Finally, both leaderships showed political flexibility. Musa was able to spread Islam and influence while maintaining a good relationship with Egypt and Morocco. Saudi Arabia balances relationships with rival countries like the US, China, Russia, and India while using sports, investment, and diplomacy to improve their previously poor image.

Both examples clearly show the human instinct to want to do something with huge amounts of wealth. They turn it into prestige, power, and legacy that stretches over generations. However, in some categories, the will doesn’t constitute an eternal power structure. Mali declined after Musa’s death, and Saudi had to scale back or completely scrap some of its projects.
