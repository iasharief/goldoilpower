Ancient World Project

Comparison

Mansa Musa’s Hajj and Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030, although separated by more than seven hundred years, display many similarities as to how leaders convert raw resource wealth into political and cultural power. While executed in differing strategies, they show how they move past limitations in geography and economy. Both examples show how wealth becomes politically influential when it is visible globally. Musa’s sixty thousand-person entourage with “500 servants, each carrying a staff made from more than 4 pounds of gold” and “100 camels–each carrying 300 pounds of gold” mirrors Saudi Arabia’s $480 million summer spending on soccer players such as Ronaldo, Benzema, Kante, and Mane. Compounded with of LIV Golf’s hundreds of millions for contracts to the best professional golfers, and the overall ten billion push that “catapult[ed] them among the biggest spenders in global football,” it is evident they are reshaping how the outside world sees them.123 They use underground riches like Mali’s gold and Saudi Arabia’s oil exports into non-tangible assets that can be used to gain “soft power”.456 This power can be used to foster trade and boost global recognition. On top of that, both empires looked to create and invest in cultural and economic hubs. Musa recruited al-Sahili to build mosques and holy sites, and laid the foundation for a university. Musa’s expenditure highlights how PIF’s (Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund) investments look to find potential, rather than keeping money around. PIF invested $100 billion for AI with Blackstone, $30 billion for EA, among EV investments.7

These investments mirror Mali’s efforts to build lasting influence and power in the future, even funding competing markets (EV vs. oil) to diversify its economy. Another similarity is that neither Mali nor Saudi Arabia turned wealth into power alone. In Saudi Arabia’s case, their sportswashing only works because other groups, such as leagues, clubs, and sports organizations, are willing to take part for their own financial gain and attention. Mansa Musa had a similar case with Egypt as a whole. Cairo's merchants and court benefited greatly from the wealth Mali brought, while Mali gained diplomatic relations, more recognition, and access to Islamic scholars.89 Finally, both leaderships showed political flexibility. Musa was able to spread Islam and influence while maintaining a good relationship with Egypt and Morocco. Saudi Arabia balances relationships with rival countries like the US, China, Russia, and India while using sports, investment, and diplomacy to improve their previously poor image.

Both examples clearly show inner pressure to use vast amounts of wealth. They turn it into prestige, power, and legacy that stretches over generations. However, in some categories, the will doesn’t constitute an eternal power structure. Mali declined after Musa’s death, and Saudi had to scale back or completely scrap some of its projects.
